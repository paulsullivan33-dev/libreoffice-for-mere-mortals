# Your First Document

The lab opened a real document in a
real window — on a screen made of
software, but the window was the
program's own — and this chapter is
written from that window, not from
imagination. A full afternoon of
interactive editing is the one thing
the lab could not rehearse for you, and
where this chapter teaches the feel of
editing rather than the facts of the
window, it says so.

## The Start Center

Start LibreOffice with no document and
you land in the **Start Center**: a
plain lobby with a menu bar of three
(File, Tools, Help), a left rail, and a
large empty area. The rail is the whole
first lesson:

- **Open File** and **Remote Files** —
  fetch an existing document, from this
  machine or from a server.
- **Recent Documents** — the ones you
  have been working on; the big area
  fills with their thumbnails.
- **Templates** — start from a prepared
  shape (chapter 11).
- **Create:** — one line per component:
  *Writer Document, Calc Spreadsheet,
  Impress Presentation, Draw Drawing,
  Math Formula, Base Database.*

Pick a face and the component opens,
empty and ready. Nothing here needs
explaining twice — which is the design.
Most days you will skip the lobby
entirely by opening a document from
your file manager; the component that
owns the format wakes up with it
loaded, as the lab's letter did.

## The document window

Open the lab's letter in Writer and
the window has the geography every
component shares, with Writer's own
furniture in the middle:

- **Menu bar** — File, Edit, View,
  Insert, Format, **Styles**, Table,
  Form, Tools, Window, Help. Two things
  to notice: Styles gets its own menu
  (chapter 5 is why), and everything
  under Tools, ending in **Options**,
  is where the program's settings live.
- **Toolbars** — two rows by default:
  the standard row (open, save, export
  as PDF, print, cut and paste) and the
  formatting row, which is *contextual
  truth-telling*: with the cursor in the
  letter's heading it reads **Heading
  1**, font **Calibri**, 14 pt. That
  style box, top left, is the most
  informative control in the program —
  it tells you what the text under your
  cursor *is*, not just how it looks.
- **The page** — the letter itself:
  heading, table with its shaded rows,
  bulleted list, one italic sentence,
  all rendered from a Word file by the
  filter you met in chapter 2.
- **The sidebar** — a narrow icon strip
  on the right edge (properties, styles,
  gallery, navigator and page panels).
  It opens panels beside the document
  instead of dialogs over it. New users
  can ignore it for weeks without loss.
- **The status bar** — along the
  bottom, and quietly the best summary
  in the window: *Page 1 of 1 · 172
  words, 974 characters · Default Page
  Style · English (USA)* · the zoom
  control. Word counts, page style and
  language for the spell-checker, all
  at a glance.

Calc and Impress rearrange the middle
— a grid of cells, a slide and its
filmstrip — and keep the frame: menus,
toolbars, sidebar, status bar. Learn
the frame once.

## Saving, and the warning that helps

Press Ctrl+S (or File → Save) on a new
document and you choose a name, a
place, and — pre-chosen for you —
**a format**. A document born as
`.docx` saves back as `.docx`; a new
one defaults to ODF (`.odt` in Writer).
Save a document *into* a foreign format
and LibreOffice shows its famous
warning: *"This document may contain
formatting or content that cannot be
saved… Use ODF Format / Use Word
Format."* It is not nagging; it is the
fidelity problem of chapter 8 arriving
early, and it offers a setting to stop
asking (Tools → Options → Load/Save).
Chapter 8 explains how to answer it
well: keep the original in the native
format, export the foreign copy when
someone needs it.

## The habit that saves afternoons

Save early, save as ODF while the work
is yours, and let the format
conversions be deliberate acts —
chapter 9's engine does them by the
folder when the time comes. The next
chapter is about what to put in the
document: words arranged by styles,
not by hand.
